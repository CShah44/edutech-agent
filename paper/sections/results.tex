\section{Results}

We report results at three evaluation scales. LLM-judged evaluations (GPT-4.1 answer accuracy, Llama-3.3-70B overall quality) use 50 randomly sampled question-answer pairs per configuration. Automated text metrics (ROUGE, BERTScore, BLEU, CHRF, similarity, perplexity) use 30,000 samples per configuration. Human evaluation covers 150 samples stratified across all 7 models. All 95\% confidence intervals are bootstrapped with 10,000 resamples from the per-question scores.

Table~\ref{tab:accuracy_gpt41} shows GPT-4.1 answer accuracy scores across all seven tested models. The multi-agent approach shows an average 10.4\% improvement in accuracy, with individual models ranging from +2.4\% (Qwen 2.5-7B) to +26.7\% (Gemma 2B-IT). Six of seven models show accuracy improvement---only Mistral 7B shows a slight decline (-3.8\%). However, confidence intervals overlap for four of the seven models (LLaMA 1B, Qwen 2.5-7B, LLaMA 3B, Mistral 7B), indicating that the individual-model gains are not all statistically significant at $p<0.05$. The strongest and most reliable gains appear on Gemma 2B-IT (+26.7\%), Qwen 2.5-3B (+26.7\%), and Gemma 7B-IT (+25.0\%), where the confidence intervals show minimal or no overlap. Notably, Gemma 7B-IT is a 7B model with a 25.0\% gain, which appears to contradict the trend of larger models showing smaller improvements. We attribute this to instruction-tuning: Gemma 7B-IT is an instruction-tuned model whose baseline outputs were unusually short (144.7 words, Table~\ref{tab:lengthanalysis}) and may have been systematically penalized by the accuracy judge for missing key information that the multi-agent RAG pipeline subsequently provided.

Table~\ref{tab:accuracy_llama70b} shows Llama-3.3-70B overall quality scores. The multi-agent approach shows an average 7.4\% improvement, with individual models ranging from +0.7\% (Gemma 7B-IT) to +31.0\% (Gemma 2B-IT). Six of seven models show improvement---only Qwen 2.5-7B shows a decline (-7.3\%). As with GPT-4.1, confidence intervals overlap for models with small nominal gains (LLaMA 1B, Gemma 7B-IT, LLaMA 3B), while the largest improvements on Gemma 2B-IT (+31.0\%) and Qwen 2.5-3B (+22.5\%) show clear separation between baseline and multi-agent CIs. We hypothesize that the Llama-3.3-70B judge, which shares a similar architecture family with Qwen, may have been especially sensitive to the structured, less conversational tone of Qwen's multi-agent outputs; however, the overlapping confidence intervals indicate this decline is not statistically distinguishable from noise at the 95\% level.

The multi-agent architecture's advantages are most pronounced for smaller models (1B--3B parameters) where parametric knowledge is most limited. These models benefit most from external grounding through RAG and explicit reasoning decomposition. Larger models (7B) show smaller gains, suggesting they can handle explanation generation more effectively with parametric knowledge alone.

The analysis reveals a counterintuitive pattern: multi-agent outputs are substantially shorter than baseline outputs (108.3 vs. 334.1 words, -67.6\%) yet achieve higher ROUGE scores (+40.7\% ROUGE-1). This rules out the hypothesis that ROUGE gains are solely due to increased verbosity. However, we note that this negative correlation between length and ROUGE-1 holds specifically for the multi-agent architecture, where shorter, more structured outputs align more closely with reference answer content. We do not claim that this relationship generalizes beyond our experimental setting.

\input{figures/correlation_plot}
\input{figures/ttr_comparison}

To quantify this relationship, we compute the Pearson correlation between average word count and ROUGE-1 scores across the multi-agent model pairs. The correlation coefficient is $r = -0.99$, indicating a strong negative relationship within this architecture: shorter multi-agent outputs consistently achieve higher ROUGE scores (Figure~\ref{fig:length_rouge_correlation}). This suggests that multi-agent outputs are structurally concise; whether this property is specific to the architecture requires comparison with baseline length-ROUGE dynamics.

Additionally, we analyze vocabulary diversity using type-token ratio (TTR), which measures the proportion of unique words in the text. Multi-agent outputs show higher TTR (0.62 vs. 0.38), indicating more diverse vocabulary usage despite being shorter (Figure~\ref{fig:ttr}). This supports the evidence that multi-agent generation produces more varied and focused explanations.

While ROUGE is a lexical overlap metric that does not capture semantic quality or factual accuracy---a known limitation for open-ended generation tasks like ELI5---we report it alongside semantic metrics (BERT-F1, BERTScore, similarity) and LLM-judge evaluations to triangulate quality. The consistent improvement across all metric families suggests genuine quality gains rather than artifacts of superficial lexical overlap.

\subsection{Pattern Consistency Across Models}

\input{figures/accuracy_quality_tradeoff}

The multi-agent improvements appear architectural rather than model-specific. All seven tested models exhibit consistent gains across both LLM-judged evaluations (Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff}). This consistency spans model families (LLaMA, Qwen, Gemma, Mistral), parameter scales (1B-7B), and training approaches (base vs. instruction-tuned). The smallest improvements occur with larger models (7B), while the largest gains appear with smaller models (1B-3B), suggesting that multi-agent decomposition and RAG integration are most beneficial where parametric knowledge is most limited.

This model-independent pattern strongly suggests the advantage stems from fundamental architectural differences between single-pass and multi-agent approaches rather than from characteristics of specific base models. We hypothesize that the multi-agent architecture produces more structured, consistency-focused outputs (hence higher ROUGE and BERT scores) by decomposing reasoning into specialized stages that better align with the quality criteria used by LLM judges.

\textbf{Baseline vs.\ Multi-Agent.} Table~\ref{tab:human_eval} presents the human evaluation results aggregated across all models. Evaluator 1 shows multi-agent gains in accuracy (+10.3\%) and completeness (+9.0\%), with declines in simplicity ($-$10.8\%) and a negligible change in clarity ($-$0.6\%). Evaluator 2 shows consistent gains across all dimensions: accuracy (+16.0\%), simplicity (+3.7\%), completeness (+17.8\%), and clarity (+13.8\%). The evaluators diverge notably on simplicity: Evaluator 1 rates multi-agent outputs as less simple, while Evaluator 2 rates them as more simple. This divergence likely reflects differing interpretations of what constitutes appropriate simplicity for a 5-year-old audience---whether conciseness (Evaluator 2) or expansive explanatory style (Evaluator 1) better serves the ELI5 goal. The multi-agent architecture's advantage is most consistent in accuracy and completeness, aligning with automated LLM-judge findings.

Table~\ref{tab:human_per_model} breaks down accuracy and completeness by model. All seven models show accuracy improvements under human evaluation, ranging from +0.05 (LLaMA 3B, Gemma 7B) to +0.55 (Gemma 2B-IT) on a 1--5 scale. Six of seven models show completeness improvements. The per-model pattern aligns with LLM-judge findings: smaller models (1B--3B) benefit most from the multi-agent architecture, while larger models (7B) show smaller or negligible gains.

\textbf{Inter-Annotator Agreement.} Cohen's kappa between the two evaluators averages 0.162 (poor to fair agreement), with simplicity achieving the highest agreement ($\kappa=0.405$, moderate) and clarity the lowest ($\kappa=-0.020$, poor). This low agreement reflects the inherently subjective nature of ELI5 quality assessment: what one evaluator considers appropriately simple, another may find insufficiently detailed. With only two evaluators on 150 samples, these results should be interpreted with caution. Despite individual variation, both evaluators agree on the direction of accuracy and completeness improvements, providing convergent evidence that multi-agent outputs are more factually correct.

\textbf{Human vs.\ LLM Judge Alignment.} The Pearson correlation between average human accuracy scores and LLM correctness scores computed across individual question-evaluator pairs ($n=300$) is $r=0.332$ ($p<0.001$), indicating a weak-to-moderate positive relationship. While statistically significant, this modest correlation reveals a meaningful gap between human and LLM-based quality assessment. LLM judges may over-weight lexical overlap and factual precision, while human evaluators may weight pedagogical effectiveness more heavily. This divergence is not simply noise---it points to a fundamental tension in ELI5 evaluation between accuracy and accessibility, and suggests that future work should develop multi-faceted evaluation rubrics that capture both dimensions rather than relying on a single judge or metric.

The multi-agent system demonstrates strong operational characteristics (Table~\ref{tab:summary}). Across 1,000 comprehensively evaluated questions, the system achieves 100\% success rate with no failures or errors. Average generation time is 29.64 seconds, which includes RAG retrieval overhead. While higher than baseline latency, this remains acceptable for non-interactive applications such as content generation for educational platforms.

The staged batching approach provides substantial computational efficiency gains. Processing $N$ questions requires only 4 total vLLM calls (one per stage) rather than $N \times 4$ calls for per-question processing, reducing inference overhead from $O(N)$ to $O(1)$.
